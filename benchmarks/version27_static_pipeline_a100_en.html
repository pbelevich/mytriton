01 · WHY V26 LEFT SPEED BEHIND

Overlap existed. Address work remained dynamic.

Version 26 hid global-memory latency, but every loop iteration still selected a stage with modulo arithmetic and reconstructed large swizzled ldmatrix address expressions.

02 · ONE STEADY-STATE GROUP

Copies, fragments, and MMA form a fixed rhythm.

The loop advances by three tiles. Each phase launches the future copy, loads two K=16 register sets, executes MMA, then waits only before shared-stage reuse.

Phase 0

cp.async K+2 → S2
ldmatrix S0 · fragments 0/1
mma.sync · K0/K1
wait_group 1 · barrier

Phase 1

cp.async K+3 → S0
ldmatrix S1 · fragments 0/1
mma.sync · K0/K1
wait_group 1 · barrier

Phase 2

cp.async K+4 → S1
ldmatrix S2 · fragments 0/1
mma.sync · K0/K1
wait_group 1 · barrier
Important nuance. A sequential fragment experiment had essentially the same ratio to torch.mm. Register buffering is part of the schedule, but address hoisting, static stages, warp-raked copies, and less cross-warp duplication explain the reliable aggregate gain.
03 · WARP-RAKED PRODUCER

Four warps divide the transport.

A warp owns 32 rows of A and eight rows of B. A lane emits four 16-byte A copies and four 16-byte B copies. Shared destinations already include the XOR swizzle.

04 · ADDRESS HOISTING

Compute once, add constants later.

Per-lane shared addresses live outside the K loop. Stage and second-fragment selection become byte additions.

2 store bases

One A and one B destination per lane.

4 A bases

One ldmatrix.x4 base per M fragment.

4 B bases

One transposed base per N-fragment pair.

2 deltas

K+16 and B row-group adjustments.

06 · NCU EVIDENCE

More compute from fewer resident warps.

The four-warp tile doubles accumulator ownership per warp and halves occupancy, yet static work reduces scheduler cost and raises SM utilization.
